Photosynthesis is the process by which green plants, algae, and some bacteria convert
light energy into chemical energy stored in glucose. It takes place mainly in the
chloroplasts, inside organelles called thylakoids and the surrounding stroma.

The overall equation is: 6 CO2 + 6 H2O + light energy -> C6H12O6 + 6 O2.

Photosynthesis has two stages. The light-dependent reactions occur in the thylakoid
membranes and split water molecules, releasing oxygen and producing ATP and NADPH.
The Calvin cycle, or light-independent reactions, takes place in the stroma and uses
ATP and NADPH to fix carbon dioxide into glucose. The key enzyme of carbon fixation is
RuBisCO, which catalyzes the attachment of CO2 to ribulose bisphosphate (RuBP).

C3 plants fix carbon directly in the Calvin cycle. C4 plants first fix CO2 into a
four-carbon compound in mesophyll cells and then release it in bundle-sheath cells,
which reduces photorespiration in hot, dry climates. CAM plants, such as cacti, open
their stomata at night to conserve water and store CO2 as malate, releasing it during
the day for the Calvin cycle.

The rate of photosynthesis depends on light intensity, carbon dioxide concentration,
and temperature. At low light, light is the limiting factor; beyond the light saturation
point, CO2 or temperature usually limits the rate.
